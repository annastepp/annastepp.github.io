Research focus

Early identification of reading difficulty is crucial to a child's development. Dyslexia, a processing difficulty affecting the acquisition of reading and spelling, commonly goes undiagnosed until a child has repeatedly failed to learn to read in elementary school, with consequences for both educational outcomes and mental health. Because this wait-to-fail approach can delay intervention until second or third grade, children miss the kindergarten window in which intensive reading interventions are most effective.

Part of the problem is how reading ability is measured. Current methods rely on one-time, static assessments that capture a snapshot of performance and miss the learning context around it. Recent evidence suggests that dynamic assessment — evaluating how a child learns over time rather than what they can do at a single moment — may predict reading difficulty better than static approaches. Operationalizing that at scale remains a challenge, since it requires repeated observation of every student rather than a single test.

This project addresses that gap by developing an interpretable machine learning early warning system built on data that schools already generate. Students complete blending drills: brief phonics exercises in which they sound out and decode words of increasing complexity. Each session leaves a record of what was attempted, what was correct, and which errors occurred. The aim is to use that ordinary practice data, collected passively while a child works, to identify students at risk of delayed decoding growth well before a formal assessment would flag them.

My responsibilities

I work with session-level performance data from elementary students, collected through a word ladder blending drill game developed within the Storiza virtual learning environment and aligned with the UFLI curriculum. My first task is feature construction — drawing signals out of raw drill interactions, including accuracy rates across sessions, the distribution of error types a student makes, how word complexity progresses for them, and the shape of their learning rate over time.

Context

Machine learning has been applied to gameplay data for this purpose before. Working with data from GraphoGame, a digital literacy application, Foldnes et al. correctly identified roughly three quarters of at-risk first graders at school entry. Their approach relied on general letter-sound matching activity rather than structured phoneme blending drills, which means the specific error types a student makes and the way word complexity progresses for them were not directly observable in the data. Using blending drills makes both visible, and that is where this project expects to add something the prior work could not.
